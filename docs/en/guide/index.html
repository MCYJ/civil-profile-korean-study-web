A common elevation datum

Use one datum when comparing elevations. Adding the same constant to every elevation leaves rises and grades unchanged.

Horizontal cumulative distance

Cumulative distance uses a shared horizontal origin. Differences give each segment’s horizontal run; do not substitute its sloping length in the grade denominator.

Units and input precision

This model accepts metres to 0.01m precision. Consistently converting both lengths from metres to centimetres leaves percentage grade unchanged.

Direction and sign

An elevation increase from A to B is positive; a decrease is negative. Establish the station order before calculating signed changes.

Segment grade

A 1m rise over 50m of horizontal run gives 100×1/50=2%. Equal elevations give 0%; this model does not calculate a segment with zero horizontal run.

Overall grade and weighting

A 1m rise over 20m followed by 1m over 80m gives 5% and 1.25%, but an overall 2%. The simple mean 3.125% is not the overall value.

A peak hidden by endpoints

Identical start and end elevations can hide an intermediate rise and fall. Read the example’s+4%/−4% segments alongside its overall 0%.

The sum of two sloping lengths

Each straight segment length uses its horizontal run and elevation change. Two lengths through a peak can differ from the direct start-to-end length.

Vertical exaggeration and angle

A 5× vertical exaggeration changes the line’s on-screen angle. Numerical grade uses the original distances and elevations, not that displayed angle.

Model scope versus design rules

Three virtual stations, two lines and input-spacing limits define this learning tool. Do not extend them to real alignment design, vertical curves, terrain corrections or current code-compliance assessment.
